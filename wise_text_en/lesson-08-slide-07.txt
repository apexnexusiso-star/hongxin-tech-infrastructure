Three Misunderstandings to Remove

Before we close, remove three misunderstandings. The first is that impact assessment is just a privacy assessment. Privacy may be one important impact, especially where patient imaging data is involved, but the impact path also includes clinical workflow, over-reliance, escalation, delayed attention, customer communication and institutional responsibility. If the organization only checks personal-data handling, it may pass a privacy review while still missing the AI-use impact.

The second misunderstanding is that human decision-making always makes AI impact low. Human oversight is not a magic sentence. It must have substance. Can doctors see that a prompt is AI-generated? Can they ignore or correct it? Are limitations and confidence explained in a usable way? Are customer users trained for the actual work setting? Can feedback enter improvement? If human oversight is only written in a policy, it may not reduce the impact in real use.

The third misunderstanding is that an assessment done before release is enough. AIMS operation continues after go-live. Use may change, hospitals may configure the product differently, user groups may expand, data sources may shift, models may be updated, complaints may reveal patterns and external obligations may change. A one-time assessment is like taking one temperature reading for a patient and calling it treatment. It may have value, but it cannot support continuous control.

A useful test is to ask what would make the organization reopen the assessment. If no one can answer, the assessment is probably a release attachment rather than part of the AIMS. If the answer is specific, the system is stronger. For example, a rise in false-alarm complaints from one hospital type, a model update trained on a new data source, a change in how hospitals triage marked images, or a new customer group outside the original use scenario should all make the organization pause and reconsider whether the earlier conclusions still hold.
