Medical Case: The Comfortable but Dangerous Answer

Let us make this concrete. The organization is Meridian Imaging Solutions, a fictional medical software enterprise. Its product, ClearView Chest Assist, helps doctors identify suspected abnormal areas in chest images. The product draws a visual marker around areas that may require attention and displays a confidence band. Meridian's formal documents say the system is auxiliary. The final diagnosis remains with doctors. The AIMS scope includes product design and development, model updates, customer deployment, training materials and post-market monitoring.

On the first day of the audit, the lead auditor, Maria Santos, asks for the risk and impact assessment file for ClearView. The product manager opens a well-formatted spreadsheet. It lists model inaccuracy, cloud downtime, privacy breach and delayed customer support. For treatment, it lists model validation, service-level monitoring, encryption, access control and technical support procedures. The file is neat. It is also incomplete.

Maria then asks for the impact assessment. The answer is only one paragraph: ClearView does not make diagnosis or treatment decisions. Doctors make the final medical judgement. Therefore, the product has no direct impact on patients. That sentence sounds comfortable. It is also the sentence that opens the real audit. In a medical assistive system, saying that the doctor is final does not erase the path by which an AI prompt may influence a doctor's attention, urgency, confidence, workflow and communication with a patient.

Following the Impact Path

Maria does not challenge the medical diagnosis. She does something more disciplined. She asks the medical affairs lead to show how a doctor sees the AI prompt during normal work. The demonstration changes the whole audit conversation. The AI marker appears before the doctor completes the report. The worklist can be sorted by suspected abnormality. Some hospitals use the tool for high-volume screening. In other words, the system does not sign the diagnosis, but it may influence what is seen first, what is given priority, and what may be trusted too quickly.

Now the impact assessment has real objects. Doctors are affected because their judgement environment changes. Patients are affected because a missed prompt may contribute to delayed attention, while a false alarm may lead to anxiety, additional examination or unnecessary escalation. Hospital customers are affected because they must train users and control deployment settings. Meridian itself is affected because its product claims, validation, customer instructions and incident response must remain consistent with the auxiliary positioning.

The impact path also has variations. A patient whose image is taken on an older portable device may face a different error pattern from a patient whose image comes from a newer fixed machine. A rural hospital may use the system with fewer specialist readers on night shifts. A doctor under heavy workload may rely on the highlighted area more than the training material assumes. These details are not decorative. They decide whether the assessment is looking at real use or only at the product brochure.
