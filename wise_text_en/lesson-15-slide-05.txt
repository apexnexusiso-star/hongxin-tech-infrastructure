Evidence Sufficiency And Finding Language

At this point, Amira must be careful. She should not write that employees leaked confidential information unless the evidence proves it. She should not write that NexaOffice is unsafe. She should not require one fixed technical solution, such as full prompt logging, if the organization has legitimate privacy constraints. The finding must stay within management-system evidence.

A stronger draft finding would sound like this: For the sampled NexaOffice AI Suite use in Project Mariner, the organization marked AI system use controls as implemented in the SoA and required employees not to input restricted information or send AI-assisted output without review. However, sampled evidence did not demonstrate that these controls operated effectively: training did not include scenario-based understanding of restricted project information, the project review record did not identify AI-assisted content or review steps, and no monitoring or sampling record was available to verify use-control implementation during the audit period.

That language is less dramatic than saying the tool is dangerous. It is also more professional. It contains criteria, sample, evidence gap, and significance. It explains why the issue matters for the AIMS without judging the quality of the AI model or prescribing a technology design. This is management-system language, and it is the language Annex A audit findings need.

Evidence sufficiency also depends on the conclusion being drawn. If Amira only wants to confirm that a policy exists, one intranet page may be enough. If she wants to conclude that use controls operated for project deliverables during the audit period, the evidence burden is higher. She needs a sample, records or logs that belong to that sample, interview consistency, and a way to understand how exceptions are detected. The broader the conclusion, the stronger the evidence chain must be.

Before finalizing the finding, Amira runs a fairness test. Would the auditee understand the criterion? Is the sampled system named clearly? Are the facts separated from interpretation? Does the evidence show a control weakness, not only an auditor preference? Does the wording stay within ISO/IEC 42001 and the organization's own arrangements? If one of these answers is weak, the finding needs more work before it is issued.

The fairness test also tells the auditor when to stop sampling. The purpose is not to investigate every NexaOffice prompt or every client memo. The purpose is to obtain enough appropriate evidence for the conclusion. If one project sample shows a possible systemic gap, the auditor may expand sampling. If expanded sampling shows the same pattern across roles or projects, the finding is stronger. If it does not, the conclusion may need to be narrower.
