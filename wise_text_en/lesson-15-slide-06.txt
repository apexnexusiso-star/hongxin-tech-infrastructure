Control Failure Diagnosis

The textbook reminds us not to stop at the missing record. A missing AI-output review record may have several possible explanations. The rule may be too general. The workflow may not have a field for AI-assisted content. Managers may not know when a record is required. The enterprise console may not provide useful monitoring. The organization may have expanded use faster than it updated its SoA and training.

The auditor may identify these as evidence directions, but the organization owns the root cause analysis. Amira can say the evidence indicates weaknesses in implementation and operating-effectiveness monitoring. She should not write the corrective action for LumenBridge. During corrective action verification, she can later evaluate whether the organization's cause analysis covers the actual failure mechanism and whether the corrective action prevents recurrence.

This is where the Annex A failure diagnosis table is useful. It does not make the finding for the auditor, and it does not replace judgement. It helps the audit team avoid a shallow statement such as records missing. In this case, the table would separate document design, implementation, evidence, dynamic maintenance, and operating effectiveness. That separation makes the later corrective action review much cleaner.

Misunderstandings To Remove

Remove three misunderstandings before we close. First, Annex A controls are not automatically mandatory in the same way for every organization. The SoA should explain applicability and exclusions based on scope, role, risk, impact, and actual AI activities. But if a control is selected, the organization must be able to show implementation evidence.

Second, a low-risk or medium-risk office tool does not mean no audit depth. Depth should be proportionate, but proportionate does not mean symbolic. For an internal assistive tool, policy, inventory, basic training and limited monitoring may sometimes be enough. When the tool affects client deliverables, contract language, confidential information, or customer commitments, the auditor should expect stronger evidence.

Third, evidence sufficiency is not a single document. A policy can be true and still insufficient. A training dashboard can be accurate and still not prove understanding. A console screenshot can show a setting and still not prove operating effectiveness over time. The audit conclusion comes from the relationship among documents, interviews, system settings, records, samples, and monitoring.
