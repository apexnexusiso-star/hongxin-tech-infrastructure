Opening Dialogue

Instructor: Hello everyone, and welcome back. In the last two lessons, we moved across Annex A control themes: policy, organization, resources, impact assessment, lifecycle, data, information for interested parties, use of AI systems, and third-party relationships. Today we test whether those themes can become real audit work.

Learner: Annex A still feels like a large checklist. If the Statement of Applicability says a control is implemented, do we just ask for the procedure and move to the next control?

Instructor: That is the tempting answer, and it is exactly the trap of this lesson. Annex A is not audited by reciting control names. A selected control must be turned into a purpose, a criterion, a sample, evidence, and finally a conclusion. Today our key phrase is the control-to-finding drill.

Why This Lesson Is A Stress Test

This lesson is deliberately less dramatic than the medical impact case. There is no emergency room, no patient pathway, and no obvious life-safety story. The subject is practical Annex A control auditing, which can easily become a dry tour through tables. If we can make this lesson concrete, the writing method is strong enough for the rest of the course.

Our textbook boundary is sections 8.11 to 8.19, supported by the generative AI office-tool case in section 12.5 and the Annex A traceability workpapers in Appendix L. By the end, you should be able to use an Annex A control audit method, identify common SoA gaps, trace one control to one AI system sample, design role-specific interview questions, and judge whether evidence is sufficient for a finding.

Listen for the movement. We start from a control theme, but we do not stay there. We move to a sampled AI system, a real use case, a responsible role, an operating record, and a conclusion that can survive report review. That movement is what separates a control checklist from an audit.

Keep that movement in your notes today.
