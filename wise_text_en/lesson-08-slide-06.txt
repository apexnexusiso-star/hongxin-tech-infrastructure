The Method Behind the Story

Now bring the story back to the textbook method. Risk management normally includes identification, analysis, evaluation and treatment. In identification, the organization finds the uncertainties that may affect responsible AI use and AIMS results. In analysis, it considers likelihood, consequences, causes, existing controls and impact paths. In evaluation, it decides whether the risk is acceptable and what priority it has. In treatment, it selects controls, changes processes, avoids some uses, transfers some responsibilities, or accepts residual risk through an appropriate role.

For AI, this work cannot belong only to the technical team. In the Meridian case, model engineers understand performance and version behavior. Medical affairs personnel understand clinical workflow and professional validation limits. Customer training personnel know what hospitals actually hear during deployment. Compliance personnel know applicable obligations and customer requirements. Technical support personnel know complaints and abnormal events. A mature AIMS brings these perspectives together, because AI risk is created where technology, data, people and use context meet.

Impact assessment uses related but more human-centered questions. Who is affected? How does the AI output influence a decision, opportunity, service, health pathway, workload or responsibility? Is the effect immediate or delayed? Can the person affected understand it, challenge it, correct it, or receive human support? Is a group exposed to a pattern that may not appear if the organization only studies average model performance? These questions make the assessment useful for control selection.

Control selection should then make sense. In the Meridian case, the answer may include clearer intended-use boundaries, stronger validation coverage, customer deployment checks, training on over-reliance, interface design that keeps the AI prompt visible but not authoritative, feedback channels for suspected errors, review triggers for repeated complaints, supplier controls for cloud services and management approval for residual risks. The auditor does not demand one universal technical solution. The auditor checks whether the chosen controls are reasonable for the identified risk and impact path, and whether they are actually implemented.
